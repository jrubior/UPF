\heading{Slide-by-slide script}
\slideguide{1}{Title}{Open with a picture of a curved relationship and ask why one constant slope can miss it.}
\slideguide{2}{Roadmap}{Preview polynomials, logarithms, and interactions. Tell students every interpretation must name the relevant value of $X$ or $Z$.}
\slideguide{3}{Last lesson}{Retrieve a partial coefficient and a joint restriction. Explain that functional form changes the question the coefficient answers.}
\slideguide{4}{Last lesson}{Use the OVB sign rule to motivate why a flexible model is not a cure for confounding.}
\slideguide{5}{Introduction}{Distinguish linear in parameters from linear in the regressor. This class uses OLS with nonlinear transformations.}
\slideguide{6}{Linear fit on a linear relationship}{Show a constant marginal effect and ask where it appears in the equation.}
\slideguide{7}{Linear fit on a nonlinear relationship}{Use a curved data-generating relationship to show why residual patterns can reveal misspecification.}
\slideguide{8}{General nonlinear population function}{Introduce $\E(Y\mid X=x)$ as the object a flexible conditional mean describes.}
\slideguide{9}{Nonlinear functions of one variable}{Ask students to calculate a derivative before interpreting a coefficient.}
\slideguide{10}{Polynomials}{Write $10+2x-.05x^2$ and calculate the marginal effect at $x=10$.}
\slideguide{11}{Polynomials}{Find the turning point $x=20$ and ask whether it lies inside the observed support.}
\slideguide{12}{Polynomials}{Calculate the exact change from 10 to 11, $.95$, and compare it with the derivative at 10.}
\slideguide{13}{Polynomials}{Explain that including $x$ and $x^2$ is still linear regression in the coefficients.}
\slideguide{14}{Log functions}{State which variable is logged before interpreting a coefficient. Never say ``one percent'' without checking the specification.}
\slideguide{15}{Log functions}{For a linear-log model, a one-unit change in $X$ is associated with approximately $100\beta_1/Y$ percent when $Y$ is in levels; use the deck's exact convention.}
\slideguide{16}{Log functions}{For a log-linear model, a one-unit change in $X$ changes $Y$ by approximately $100\beta_1$ percent.}
\slideguide{17}{Log functions}{For a log-log model, interpret $\beta_1$ as an elasticity: a 1 percent change in $X$ is associated with about $\beta_1$ percent in $Y$.}
\slideguide{18}{Log-outcome example}{Use $.10$ to calculate the exact change $100(e^{.10}-1)\simeq10.52\%$ and compare it with the approximation.}
\slideguide{19}{Log-outcome example}{Ask students to identify the units of the coefficient and the reference change in $X$.}
\slideguide{20}{Log-outcome example}{Warn that log interpretations are not causal without a credible identifying design.}
\slideguide{21}{Comparing log specifications}{Have students translate each equation into a sentence before comparing the coefficients.}
\slideguide{22}{Interactions}{Explain why the effect of one variable can depend on another.}
\slideguide{23}{Two dummies}{Write the four predicted group means and identify the omitted reference group.}
\slideguide{24}{Two dummies}{Use the interaction coefficient as a difference-in-differences contrast in the descriptive model.}
\slideguide{25}{Two dummies}{Ask whether the interaction coefficient is a causal DiD effect; answer only with the required design assumptions.}
\slideguide{26}{Dummy and continuous variable}{For $Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\beta_2D+\beta_3XD$, calculate the group contrast at $X=4$.}
\slideguide{27}{Dummy and continuous variable}{Use the example $3+4x$ for the program effect and explain why 3 is only the effect at $x=0$.}
\slideguide{28}{Dummy and continuous variable}{Calculate the slope for each group: $\beta_1$ and $\beta_1+\beta_3$.}
\slideguide{29}{Dummy and continuous variable}{Test the effect at $x=4$ as a linear restriction, not by testing only $\beta_2$.}
\slideguide{30}{Dummy and continuous variable}{Ask how centering $X$ changes the interpretation of the main dummy coefficient.}
\slideguide{31}{Dummy and continuous variable}{Show that centering changes parameterization but not fitted values.}
\slideguide{32}{Continuous interactions}{Interpret $\partial\E(Y\mid X,Z)/\partial X=\beta_1+\beta_3Z$.}
\slideguide{33}{Continuous interactions}{Give a numerical example with $Z=2$ and $Z=5$ so students see the slope move.}
\slideguide{34}{Continuous interactions}{Remind students to report the value of the interacting variable with every marginal effect.}
\slideguide{35}{Continuous interactions}{Discuss extrapolation: do not interpret effects far outside the observed support.}
\slideguide{36}{Continuous interactions}{Connect conditional effects to joint hypothesis tests for several coefficients.}
\slideguide{37}{Wrapping up}{Summarize polynomial, log, and interaction interpretations in a three-row board table.}
\slideguide{38}{Wrapping up}{Ask which models remain linear in parameters and can be estimated by OLS.}
\slideguide{39}{Wrapping up}{Use one final translation exercise and insist on units.}
\slideguide{40}{Wrapping up}{Tie functional form back to fit, prediction, and causal interpretation.}
\slideguide{41}{Linear versus non-linear models}{Close with the distinction between a nonlinear conditional mean and a nonlinear estimator.}
\slideguide{42}{Importance of interactions}{Have students state one setting where a treatment effect should plausibly vary across people.}

\heading{Worked example | Quadratic experience}
\cue{Use with slides}{Lecture7v2 slides 8--13: Nonlinear Functions and Polynomials in $X$. Use the derivative and turning-point calculation on slides 9--13; pause before moving to logs.}
For the synthetic fitted relationship
\[
\widehat{wage}=10+2x-.05x^2,
\qquad \frac{\partial\widehat{wage}}{\partial x}=2-.1x,
\]
the marginal effect is 1 at $x=10$ and 0 at $x=20$. The turning point is 20. The exact fitted change from 10 to 11 is
\[
2-.05(11^2-10^2)=.95,
\]
not exactly the derivative at 10. Only discuss the turning point substantively if it lies within a relevant supported range.
\cue{Board comment}{The model is nonlinear in $x$ but linear in the coefficients. OLS can estimate it after including both $x$ and $x^2$.}

\heading{Worked examples | Logs and heterogeneous effects}
\cue{Use with slides}{Lecture7v2 slides 14--21 for the log examples and slides 22--39 for interactions. Use the log-outcome calculation on slides 18--21, then use the program-by-experience example on slides 26--38.}
If a log-outcome model has coefficient $.08$, a one-unit increase changes the fitted log index by $.08$. The corresponding multiplicative change is
\[
100(e^{.08}-1)\simeq8.33\%,
\]
versus the 8\% approximation. Exponentiating a fitted conditional log mean does not automatically give the conditional arithmetic mean of $Y$; retransformation requires attention to the error distribution.

For a level-log model with coefficient 20, a 10\% increase in $X$ implies $20\log(1.10)\simeq1.906$ outcome units, compared with the approximation 2.
\heading{Interaction board example}
Let $D$ be a program indicator and $x$ be experience:
\[
\widehat Y=10+2x+3D+1.5(xD).
\]
For $D=0$, the line is $10+2x$; for $D=1$, it is $13+3.5x$. The program-group contrast at experience $x$ is $3+1.5x$, so it equals 9 at $x=4$.
\cue{Ask}{Is 3 the program effect for everyone? No, it is the contrast at $x=0$. Is 1.5 the entire program effect? No, it is the difference in slopes. A causal reading still needs identification assumptions.}
\cue{Test at $x=4$}{Test $\beta_D+4\beta_{xD}=0$, using the covariance of the two estimates. Testing only the main dummy coefficient does not answer this question.}
\cue{Continuous interaction}{For $Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X+\beta_2Z+\beta_3XZ+u$, the $X$ derivative is $\beta_1+\beta_3Z$. State the value of $Z$ whenever reporting it.}
\cue{Exit answers}{A log-log slope of $-.7$ implies roughly a $.7$\% decrease for a 1\% increase in $X$. A quadratic model can be estimated by OLS. An interaction coefficient is not a universal total effect.}
\cue{If short}{Use one example of each functional form and one interaction in depth; assign repetitive interaction plots for self-study. Proposed Midterm 2 coverage is Lectures 5--7, using prerequisites from Lectures 1--4.}
