\heading{Session 4 | A line, an estimator, an assumption}
\textbf{Outcomes:} Compute an OLS line, distinguish errors from residuals, interpret slope units, and state the role of conditional mean zero.
\cue{Opening}{A line can summarize an association. What extra argument would let us call its slope a causal effect? Keep that question on the board throughout the algebra.}

\heading{Slide-by-slide script}
\slideguide{1}{Title}{Open with the question: what does the slope of a line summarize, and when could it be causal?}
\slideguide{2}{Roadmap}{Preview the sequence: population model, OLS calculation, interpretation, fit, and causal assumptions.}
\slideguide{3}{Last lesson}{Retrieve the difference between a population parameter and a sample statistic. Ask where the sampling variation enters.}
\slideguide{4}{Last lesson}{Use the conditional-mean table to remind students that conditioning can change the average outcome.}
\slideguide{5}{The regression line}{Draw a scatterplot and a line. Ask what feature of the cloud the slope is trying to summarize.}
\slideguide{6}{The regression line}{Explain that the fitted line predicts the conditional mean only under the population linear regression interpretation.}
\slideguide{7}{Population linear regression model}{Write $Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i$ and label the systematic part and the error.}
\slideguide{8}{OLS estimator}{Introduce the sum of squared residuals. Example: compare candidate intercepts for data $1,2,6$.}
\slideguide{9}{OLS estimator}{Differentiate the objective to obtain the first-order conditions. Circle $\sum\hat u_i=0$ and $\sum X_i\hat u_i=0$.}
\slideguide{10}{Back to the model}{Distinguish the unobserved population error $u_i$ from the computed residual $\hat u_i$.}
\slideguide{11}{Interpretation}{Interpret the slope in units. For test score on class size, a slope of $-2.3$ means 2.3 fewer points per additional student, as an association.}
\slideguide{12}{Goodness of fit}{Define fitted values and residuals. Ask whether a small residual proves a causal relationship.}
\slideguide{13}{$R^2$}{Calculate $R^2=1-SSR/TSS$ using the three-point example. Explain that in-sample fit is not identification.}
\slideguide{14}{SER}{Compute $SER=\sqrt{SSR/(n-2)}$ for a simple regression with an intercept. Explain its units.}
\slideguide{15}{Goodness of fit again}{Use the same data to show that high $R^2$ can coexist with a noncausal relation.}
\slideguide{16}{Conditions for causality}{List the causal question, a credible source of variation, and the assumptions linking the variation to the effect.}
\slideguide{17}{Conditions for causality}{Ask students to name one omitted determinant of test scores correlated with class size. Keep the answer on the board.}
\slideguide{18}{Conditional mean zero}{Interpret $\E(u_i\mid X_i)=0$ as no systematic omitted determinants left in the error at any $X$.}
\slideguide{19}{Conditional mean zero}{Use a simple violation: wealth affects both class size and test resources. Ask which direction of bias is plausible.}
\slideguide{20}{i.i.d. draws}{Explain what independent and identically distributed mean separately. Give a warning about repeated observations from the same school.}
\slideguide{21}{No large outliers}{Show why a single extreme $X$ can dominate the denominator in the slope formula. Ask whether dropping it is automatically legitimate.}
\slideguide{22}{OLS sample properties}{Separate finite-sample unbiasedness from consistency and large-sample normality.}
\slideguide{23}{Expected slope}{Use $\hat\beta_1-\beta_1=\sum(X_i-\bar X)u_i/\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2$ to point to the source of bias.}
\slideguide{24}{Variance of slope}{Explain that more spread in $X$ improves precision, while more noise in $u$ reduces it.}
\slideguide{25}{Variance calculation}{Keep the algebra secondary. Ask students to identify the numerator and denominator intuitively.}
\slideguide{26}{Variance calculation}{Use a units check: changing $X$ from students to tens of students changes the numerical slope and its standard error consistently.}
\slideguide{27}{Large-sample distribution}{Connect the CLT idea to a regression coefficient: standardized estimation error becomes approximately normal under assumptions.}
\slideguide{28}{Material}{Point students to the chapter and assign the three-point OLS calculation as the first practice problem.}

\heading{Board example | Compute, do not memorize}
\cue{Use with slides}{Lecture3v2 slides 4--9: The regression line, Population Linear Regression Model, and OLS Estimator. Begin the three-point calculation when slide 7 introduces OLS; use slides 8--9 for the first-order conditions and residual identities.}
Use synthetic observations $(x,y)=(1,2),(2,3),(3,5)$.
\[
\bar x=2,\quad \bar y=10/3,\quad
\hat\beta_1=\frac{\sum(x_i-\bar x)(y_i-\bar y)}
{\sum(x_i-\bar x)^2}=\frac3{2}=1.5.
\]
\[
\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x=1/3.
\]
Predictions are $11/6,10/3,29/6$; residuals are $1/6,-1/3,1/6$. Their sum is zero, and $\sum x_i\hat u_i=0$.
\cue{Derivation cue}{Differentiate $\sum(y_i-b_0-b_1x_i)^2$ with respect to both parameters. The first equation puts the fitted line through $(\bar x,\bar y)$; substitute it into the second to obtain the slope formula.}
\cue{Distinction}{Population $u_i$ is unobserved. Sample $\hat u_i=y_i-\hat y_i$ is computed. OLS residual orthogonality is an algebraic identity, not evidence of exogeneity.}

\heading{Use the same example | Fit and interpretation}
\cue{Use with slides}{Lecture3v2 slides 11--15: Interpretation, Goodness of Fit, $R^2$, SER, and Goodness of Fit again. Use the same three observations to compute $R^2$ on slide 13 and SER on slide 14.}
\[
SSR=\sum\hat u_i^2=1/6,\qquad
TSS=\sum(y_i-\bar y)^2=14/3.
\]
Here SSR means residual sum of squares; always define the notation because some texts use it for regression sum of squares.
\[
R^2=1-\frac{1/6}{14/3}=\frac{27}{28}\simeq.9643,
\qquad SER=\sqrt{\frac{1/6}{3-2}}=\sqrt{1/6}\simeq.408.
\]
These three observations demonstrate arithmetic, not reliable asymptotic inference.
\cue{Units exercise}{If $y$ is a test score and $x$ is class size, slope 1.5 means an associated 1.5-point difference per additional student. If $x$ is divided by 10, the numerical slope multiplies by 10. Predictions do not change.}
\heading{Causality and sampling}
\cue{Use with slides}{Lecture3v2 slides 16--26: conditions for causality, conditional mean zero, i.i.d. draws, outliers, and OLS sample properties. Use the bias expression beside slides 18--19 before moving to large-sample distribution.}
For $Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i$, a zero conditional mean $\E(u_i\mid X_i)=0$ says omitted determinants average to zero at each regressor value. Richer districts may have both different class sizes and different resources, violating this condition.
\[
\hat\beta_1-\beta_1=
\frac{\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)u_i}{\sum_i(X_i-\bar X)^2}.
\]
Use the expression to explain why variation in $X$ is needed and why correlation between $X$ and omitted determinants is a problem. Discuss random sampling and finite-moment conditions before invoking large-sample normality.
\cue{Side question}{Does a high $R^2$ establish causality? No. Does a low $R^2$ rule out a causal effect? No. Fit and identification answer different questions.}
\cue{Exit answers}{Residuals sum to zero when an intercept is included. That fact cannot verify $\E(u\mid X)=0$. The intercept may have no sensible substantive interpretation when $X=0$ is outside the observed range.}
\cue{If short / preparation}{Assign the detailed asymptotic variance algebra for review. Preserve the numerical OLS calculation and exogeneity discussion. Read Chapter 5 before session 5.}
