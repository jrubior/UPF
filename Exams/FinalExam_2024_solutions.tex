    \item State the Central Limit Theorem for a sequence of i.i.d. random variables carefully stating any conditions required. Briefly explain the theorem's importance for statistical inference.\\[1em]
\adjustbox{minipage=\textwidth,cfbox=black}{
~\\ {\color{blue} \large If $y_1, y_2, ..., y_n$ are drawn randomly from the same population (i.i.d.) and large outliers are unlikely, then $\overline{Y} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_Y, \sigma^2_{\overline{Y}})$ as $n$ grows.\\

That is, regardless of the population's distribution, the sampling distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution as the sample size increases, provided the samples are independent and identically distributed. This is powerful because it allows statisticians to use the well-understood properties of the normal distribution to estimate population parameters, calculate confidence intervals, and perform hypothesis tests, even when the original data does not follow a normal distribution and if the overall population is unobserved.
~\\
~\\
~\\

}

}\\[1em]


    \item  In panel regression, why is it important to use clustered standard errors for the regression? Do the regression results change if you do not use clustered standard errors? How? \\[1em]
\adjustbox{minipage=\textwidth,cfbox=black}{
~\\ {\color{blue} \large  In Panel Data models, we generally assume independence across units but not within them. If \( u_{i,t} \) is correlated over time (serial correlation), standard OLS standard errors -- whether homoskedasticity-only or heteroskedasticity-robust -- become invalid as they assume no such correlation. This often understates true sampling uncertainty because serially correlated errors reduce the amount of independent information. \\

As a consequence, the regression coefficients remain unchanged, the standard errors are incorrect. Clustered standard errors resolve this by accounting for dependencies within units. This reasoning can be further extend to clusters beyond the unit of analysis.
~\\
~\\

}

}\\[1em]

    \item What is sample selection bias? Suppose you read a study using data on college graduates on the effects of an additional year of schooling on earnings. What is the potential sample selection bias present?\\[1em]
\adjustbox{minipage=\textwidth,cfbox=black}{
~\\ {\color{blue} \large Sample selection bias occurs when the process influencing data availability is related to the dependent variable, beyond its dependence on the regressors. In the above example, no inherent bias arises from focusing on college graduates to the extent they form a subset based on the regressor (i.e., 16 years of schooling or more). However, a potential bias can emerge because earnings are observed only for those with a job. This bias stems from overlooking the effect of an additional year of schooling on employment probability.\\

In general, if the study aimed to estimate the returns to education for the general population, and not only for college graduates, the sample would be even more biased due to the selection of employed individuals, who are likely to be wealthier and have better employment prospects.
~\\
}

}\\[1em]


    \item Carefully explain the difference between internal validity and external validity. Is it possible for an empirical study to have internal validity but not external validity? Explain. \\[1em]
\adjustbox{minipage=\textwidth,cfbox=black}{
{\color{blue} \large A statistical analysis is said to have internal validity if the statistical inferences about causal effects are valid for the population being studied. The analysis is said to have external validity if its inferences and conclusions can be generalized from the population and setting studied to other populations and settings.\\

It is possible for a study to have internal validity but not external validity, e.g., when a study is conducted in a highly specific setting that isolates causal effects well but does not represent the broader population or other contexts.
